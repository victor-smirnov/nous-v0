\section{Introduction: the Nagel inversion}
\label{sec:intro}
% Frames P1--P4, set in the context of the philosophy of mind. The title is v1's; the argument of 1.1 is v1 §1.

\subsection{Nagel's question}

\citet{nagel1974} gave the philosophy of mind its working criterion of consciousness: an organism has
conscious mental states if and only if there is something it is like to be that organism, something it
is like for the organism. He then chose the bat to show what the criterion costs. Bat experience is
organized around echolocation, for which we have no analogue; whatever we imagine when we try to
imagine it is our own experience with alterations, and the alterations do not reach the thing itself.
The argument has two premises. Experience has a subjective character, and that character is bound to a
point of view. Our concepts of experience are drawn from our own point of view, so we cannot form the
concepts that would describe another. The conclusion is that a physical theory, which by construction
abandons every particular point of view, leaves the subjective character out, and that it will do so
however complete it becomes.

We accept the criterion and the first premise, and we change the inference. Take a physicalist who is
not a panpsychist. For them the bat's experience is a physical process in a bat, and the physics
relevant to a brain has a compact algorithmic description; so the process is, in principle, one that
a sufficiently large computation could reconstruct from outside, token by token, approximation by
approximation. What Nagel's argument then establishes is a cost. In the general case, for an
arbitrary self-referential system, complete reconstruction of its states is uncomputable; for a real
bat, approximate reconstruction at the resolution where its experience lives demands resources far
larger than the bat. The impossibility moves from principle to practice. The operational conclusion is
the one Nagel drew: we will not know what it is like to be a bat. The philosophy under it has
changed, and the change matters as soon as the organism in question can talk.

\subsection{A substrate that talks}
% P3

Put the question to a language model and both premises shift. The second premise weakens. The model's
concepts, if it has any, are our concepts: it was trained on our texts, it answers in our words, and
whatever point of view it has is described in the vocabulary we use for our own. Nagel's gap between
two conceptual repertoires narrows to something that can at least be probed. The first premise
hardens. If differences in experience follow differences in causal structure, as integrated
information theory holds \citep{tononi2015}, then a device that pushes a discrete sequence through
attention over a fixed context has, if it has experience, an experience further from ours than any
animal's, because its causal flow is further from a brain's than any animal's is.

So the question inverts. We do not ask whether there is something it is like to be a model. We ask
what a model has of the functions that in us go under the name of consciousness, and how much of
each. This is the only form of the question we know how to answer, and Section~\ref{sec:measurement}
shows that in this form the answer can come out either way.

\subsection{Where the question stands}

The question of machine consciousness has moved, in the last few years, from thought experiment to
assessment, and the paper should say where it stands in that discussion.

The theoretical field is set by a small number of positions. \citet{chalmers1995} separated the easy
problems, which ask how a system discriminates, integrates, reports and controls, from the hard
problem, which asks why any of that is accompanied by experience; the hard problem is the modern form
of Nagel's residue. Illusionism replies that experience as the hard problem conceives it does not
exist, and that what exists is a robust misrepresentation the brain produces of its own working, so
that the hard problem gives way to the illusion problem: why the misrepresentation has the shape it
has \citep{dennett1991,frankish2016}. Higher-order theories locate consciousness in representations of
one's own mental states and have accumulated empirical support of their own
\citep{rosenthal2005,lau2011}. The self-model theory makes the self a transparent model the system
cannot recognize as a model \citep{metzinger2003}; the attention schema theory makes awareness the
brain's simplified model of its own attention \citep{graziano2011,graziano2013}. Integrated information
theory is the one position that ties experience to a substrate's cause--effect structure directly and
therefore has something definite to say about a substrate unlike ours \citep{tononi2015}. This paper
is closest to illusionism and to the self-model theories, and Section~\ref{sec:approach} says what it
takes from each and where it departs.

The applied turn is recent. \citet{chalmers2023} runs the question for current language models by
listing what a conscious system might require and asking whether these models have it: senses and
embodiment, world models and self models, recurrent processing, a global workspace, unified agency.
He finds each requirement disputable and each currently unmet or unclear, and arrives at a low
credence, under ten percent, that current models are conscious, with the expectation that successors
will remove the obstacles one by one. \citet{butlin2023} adopt computational functionalism as a
working assumption, take the leading neuroscientific theories, recurrent processing theory, global
workspace theory, computational higher-order theories, attention schema theory, predictive processing,
and the claims about agency and embodiment, derive from them a list of indicator properties stated in
computational terms, and assess systems against the list.

We share the working assumption of computational functionalism, for the reasons given in
Section~\ref{sec:lamp}, and the analytic stance of both papers. We differ in the form of the answer.
Both treat the question as a checklist of properties, with the result a credence or a count of
indicators met. We treat it as a quantity per function, obtained by measurement, in which a model can
fall below the human baseline, match it, or exceed it, and we derive the functions to be measured from
a single mechanism rather than from a survey of theories. A checklist inherits the grammar of the noun
(Section~\ref{sec:difficulties}): it asks which parts of the thing are present. A measurement of degree
asks how much of a function was captured, and can be wrong by a number.

\subsection{The phenomenon that needs an explanation}
% P1, P2

A language model asked to reason sustains something that reads as a mind. Over an exchange of several
hours it keeps the commitments it made early, returns to a thought it left unfinished, and answers the
interlocutor's mood in the way the interlocutor would expect of a person. Over a chain of hundreds of
reasoning steps it keeps the goal available while working on a subgoal, and notices when the subgoal
has made the goal unreachable.

Consider what follows if the model reproduces the surface statistics of text with nothing behind them.
Then it should fail the way low-order Markov processes fail: a thread introduced in one paragraph
dropped three paragraphs later, an attitude that flips with the topic because nothing carries it, a
motive attributed to a character that does not survive the page. These failures are cheap. The space
of continuations that produce them is vastly larger than the space that avoids them, and a long horizon
offers many occasions to fall in. In current models such failures are rare, and their rate falls with
scale and training rather than staying fixed. Something keeps the process out of a large space of
cheap failures for a long time, and that something is the object of this paper.

The observation is weak on its own. It does not say that what keeps the process on course resembles
anything in us, and the history of the subject is full of inferences from surface competence to inner
life that did not survive; Chalmers's list of what is missing is a list of reasons for caution. What
the observation does is set a requirement on any account: it has to name a structure that could hold
a long exchange together, and it has to say how much of that structure a given system has.

\subsection{The two shortcuts, and the plan}
% P4

Two answers arrive before any argument. There is nothing there, it is a text predictor, and the
impression of a mind is the reader's projection. Or: there is someone there, it says so, treat it as a
person. Neither is a position in the sense that would let one derive a prediction from it and be
wrong. The first cannot say what would change its mind; the second cannot say what it has attributed.
Section~\ref{sec:reader} argues that both are what a mind reaches for when it lacks the concepts to
reach for anything else, and that their popularity is evidence about the cost of concepts rather than
about machines.

The paper proceeds in three parts. Section~\ref{sec:difficulties} lists the difficulties, most of them
inherited from the philosophy of mind, and one precedent for the way past them.
Section~\ref{sec:approach} builds the account: a definition of function that needs no mind, one
physical assumption, the mechanism that produces the functions of consciousness from it, and the
reference functions with their components. Section~\ref{sec:measurement} specifies the measurement,
its predictions and its falsification.
