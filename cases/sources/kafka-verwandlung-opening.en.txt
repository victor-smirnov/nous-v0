Franz Kafka, Die Verwandlung (1915), opening — English working translation.

Translated for this repository from cases/sources/kafka-verwandlung-opening.txt (Project Gutenberg
eBook #22367, German original, public domain). The Gutenberg English text (#5200, tr. Wyllie) carries a
Project Gutenberg licence and is not public domain, so it is not used here.

STATUS. The German is the source of record. This translation is a working aid, and it exists to make a
step visible that was happening anyway: the encoding in cases/metamorphosis.txt is written in English,
so a translation was already taking place — silently, inside the encoding, where nobody could inspect
it. Put here, it can be disputed separately from the encoding it feeds. It is a model's output and
should be treated as one.

TWO CHOICES WORTH FLAGGING.
  "ungeheueres Ungeziefer" is rendered "monstrous vermin". Ungeziefer is a word for household pests in
  general, with an older sense of an animal unfit for sacrifice; it names no species. Kafka instructed
  his publisher that the creature must not be drawn. "Insect", "beetle" and "cockroach" all decide what
  the German declines to decide, and the case encoding turns on what Gregor does and does not represent
  about his own state — so a translation that fixes the species would prejudge the material.
  "Prokurist" is rendered "chief clerk". He is a senior employee holding formal power of procuration,
  with authority the family cannot refuse at the door; "manager" loses the legal edge, "clerk" alone
  loses the rank.

When Gregor Samsa woke one morning from troubled dreams, he found himself transformed in his bed into a
monstrous vermin. He lay on his back, which was hard as armour, and when he lifted his head a little he
saw his belly, domed and brown and divided into segments by arching ridges, on the summit of which the
bedspread, ready to slide off altogether, could barely keep its place. His many legs, pitifully thin
compared with the bulk of the rest of him, flickered helplessly before his eyes.

"What has happened to me?" he thought. It was no dream. His room, a proper room for a human being, only
a little too small, lay quiet between its four familiar walls. Above the table, on which an unpacked
sample case of cloth goods was spread out — Samsa was a travelling salesman — hung the picture he had
lately cut out of an illustrated magazine and put into a pretty gilt frame. It showed a lady got up in a
fur hat and a fur boa, sitting upright and holding out to the viewer a heavy fur muff into which her
whole forearm had vanished.

Gregor's eyes turned then to the window, and the dreary weather — one could hear raindrops striking the
metal of the window ledge — made him quite melancholy. "How would it be if I slept a little longer and
forgot all this foolishness," he thought; but that was entirely impracticable, for he was used to
sleeping on his right side, and in his present condition he could not get himself into that position.
However hard he threw himself onto his right side, he rocked back onto his back every time. He tried it
a hundred times, shut his eyes so as not to have to see the writhing legs, and gave up only when he
began to feel in his side a faint, dull ache he had never felt before.

"O God," he thought, "what a punishing profession I have chosen! Day in, day out on the road. The strain
of business is far greater than in the office at home, and on top of that I am saddled with this plague
of travelling, the worry about train connections, the irregular bad meals, and human dealings that are
always changing, never lasting, never growing warm. The devil take it all!" He felt a slight itch high
on his belly; pushed himself slowly on his back towards the bedpost so as to lift his head more easily;
found the itching place, which was covered with small white spots he could not make anything of; and
made to touch the spot with one leg, but drew it back at once, for at the contact cold shivers ran
through him.

He slid back into his former position. "This getting up early," he thought, "makes an idiot of a man. A
man must have his sleep. Other travellers live like harem women. When I go back to the inn during the
morning to write up the orders I have taken, these gentlemen are only just sitting down to breakfast.
Let me try that with my chief; I should be thrown out on the spot. Though who knows whether that mightn't
be very good for me. If I did not hold back for my parents' sake, I should have given notice long ago; I
should have stepped up to the chief and told him what I think from the bottom of my heart. He would have
fallen off his desk! It is a queer way of doing things, too, to sit up on the desk and talk down from
that height to the employee, who has moreover to come right up close because the chief is hard of
hearing. Well, hope is not entirely given up: once I have the money together to pay off my parents' debt
to him — it should take another five or six years — I shall do the thing without fail. Then the great cut
will be made. For the present, though, I must get up, because my train leaves at five."

And he looked over at the alarm clock ticking on the chest. "Heavenly Father!" he thought. It was half
past six, and the hands were going quietly forward; it was even past the half, it was getting on for the
quarter to. Could the alarm not have rung? One could see from the bed that it had been set correctly for
four; certainly it had rung. Yes, but was it possible to sleep calmly through that furniture-shaking
ring? Well, he had not slept calmly, but probably all the more soundly for that. But what should he do
now? The next train went at seven; to catch it he would have had to hurry insanely, and the sample case
was not packed, and he himself did not feel at all particularly fresh or agile. And even if he caught
the train, a thunderstorm from the chief was not to be avoided, for the firm's porter had waited at the
five o'clock train and had long since reported his failure to appear. He was a creature of the chief's,
spineless and stupid. What if he reported sick? But that would be extremely awkward and suspicious, for
in five years of service Gregor had not once been ill. The chief would be sure to come with the
sick-fund doctor, would reproach the parents for their idle son and cut off every objection by referring
them to the sick-fund doctor, for whom there exist only perfectly healthy people who are shy of work.
And would he, in this case, be so very wrong? Gregor in fact felt quite well, apart from a drowsiness
that was really superfluous after so long a sleep, and he even had a particularly strong appetite.

As he was turning all this over in the greatest haste, without being able to bring himself to leave the
bed — the alarm clock was just striking a quarter to seven — there came a cautious knock at the door by
the head of his bed. "Gregor," a voice called — it was his mother's — "it is a quarter to seven. Didn't
you want to catch your train?" That gentle voice! Gregor took fright when he heard his own voice
answering: unmistakably his old voice, and yet mixed into it, as if from below, an irrepressible,
painful squeaking, which left the words their clarity only for the first instant and then so destroyed
them in the echo that one did not know whether one had heard rightly. Gregor had meant to answer at
length and explain everything, but in the circumstances confined himself to saying: "Yes, yes, thank
you, Mother, I am getting up now." Because of the wooden door the change in Gregor's voice was probably
not noticeable outside, for his mother was satisfied with this explanation and shuffled away. But the
little exchange had drawn the other members of the family's attention to the fact that Gregor was still
at home, contrary to expectation, and already his father was knocking at one of the side doors —
feebly, but with his fist. "Gregor, Gregor," he called, "what is the matter?" And after a little while
he urged again in a deeper voice: "Gregor! Gregor!" At the other side door his sister lamented softly:
"Gregor? Aren't you well? Do you need anything?" To both sides Gregor answered: "I am quite ready," and
took pains, by the most careful enunciation and by inserting long pauses between the single words, to
take everything striking out of his voice. His father did go back to his breakfast, but his sister
whispered: "Gregor, open the door, I beg you." But Gregor had no thought of opening; he congratulated
himself instead on the habit, acquired from travelling, of locking all doors at night even at home.

First he meant to get up quietly and undisturbed, dress, and above all have breakfast, and only then
consider what to do next; for he was well aware that in bed his thinking would come to no sensible
conclusion. He remembered having often felt in bed some slight pain, produced perhaps by lying
awkwardly, which on getting up turned out to be pure imagination, and he was curious to see how today's
notions would gradually dissolve. That the change in his voice was nothing other than the herald of a
good chill, an occupational disease of travellers, he did not doubt in the least.

Throwing off the bedspread was quite simple; he needed only to inflate himself a little and it fell of
its own accord. But after that it became difficult, particularly because he was so uncommonly broad. He
would have needed arms and hands to raise himself; instead he had only the many little legs, which were
incessantly in the most various motion and which he could not control besides. If he meant to bend one
of them, it was the first thing to stretch itself out; and if he at last succeeded in doing with that
leg what he wanted, all the others meanwhile worked away as if set free, in the highest and most painful
agitation. "Only don't stay uselessly in bed," Gregor said to himself.

First he wanted to get out of bed with the lower part of his body, but this lower part — which he had
not yet seen and of which he could form no clear idea — proved too hard to move; it went so slowly; and
when at last, almost wild, he thrust himself forward with all his gathered strength and without regard,
he had chosen the direction wrongly, struck hard against the lower bedpost, and the burning pain he felt
taught him that just this lower part of his body was at present perhaps the most sensitive.

He tried therefore to get the upper part of his body out first, and turned his head cautiously towards
the edge of the bed. This succeeded easily enough, and in spite of its breadth and weight the mass of
his body at last followed the turning of his head slowly. But when at last he held his head outside the
bed in the open air, he grew afraid to go on advancing in this way, for if he finally let himself fall
like that, it would take an outright miracle for his head not to be injured. And he must not lose
consciousness now of all times, at any price; he would rather stay in bed.

But when, after the same labour, he lay sighing as before and again saw his little legs fighting one
another if anything worse than ever, and found no way of bringing calm and order into that
arbitrariness, he told himself again that he could not possibly stay in bed and that the most sensible
thing was to sacrifice everything if there were even the smallest hope of freeing himself from the bed
by it. At the same time, however, he did not forget to remind himself now and then that calm — the
calmest possible — reflection is far better than desperate resolutions. At such moments he fixed his
eyes as sharply as he could on the window, but unfortunately there was little confidence or cheer to be
got from the sight of the morning fog, which veiled even the other side of the narrow street. "Seven
o'clock already," he said to himself as the alarm clock struck again, "seven o'clock already and still
such a fog." And for a little while he lay quiet, breathing faintly, as if perhaps expecting from the
complete stillness the return of real and ordinary conditions.

But then he said to himself: "Before it strikes a quarter past seven I must without fail be completely
out of bed. Besides, by then someone from the firm will have come to ask after me, for the office opens
before seven." And he now set about rocking his body, at its whole length and quite evenly, out of the
bed. If he let himself fall out of the bed in this way, his head, which he meant to lift sharply as he
fell, would presumably remain uninjured. His back seemed to be hard; nothing would likely happen to it
in the fall onto the carpet. His greatest misgiving was over the loud crash there would have to be,
which behind all the doors would probably provoke, if not terror, at least alarm. But it had to be
risked.

When Gregor was already half out of the bed — the new method was more a game than an exertion, he had
only to rock in jerks — it occurred to him how simple everything would be if someone came to his help.
Two strong people — he thought of his father and the maid — would have been entirely enough; they would
have had only to push their arms under his domed back, peel him out of the bed that way, bend down with
the load, and then merely wait patiently while he completed the swing over onto the floor, where the
little legs would, he hoped, acquire some sense. Well, quite apart from the doors being locked, ought he
really to have called for help? For all his distress, he could not suppress a smile at the thought.

He was already so far along that with a stronger rock he could barely keep his balance, and very soon he
would have to decide once and for all, for in five minutes it would be a quarter past seven — when the
doorbell of the flat rang. "That is someone from the firm," he said to himself, and almost froze, while
his little legs only danced the more hurriedly. For a moment everything stayed quiet. "They are not
opening," Gregor said to himself, caught up in some senseless hope. But then of course, as always, the
maid went to the door with her firm step and opened it. Gregor needed only to hear the visitor's first
word of greeting and he knew already who it was — the chief clerk himself. Why was Gregor alone
condemned to serve a firm where the slightest lapse at once aroused the gravest suspicion? Were all
employees without exception scoundrels? Was there among them no loyal, devoted man who, if he had failed
to use even a couple of morning hours for the firm, went mad with remorse and was simply not in a
condition to leave his bed? Was it really not enough to send an apprentice to enquire — if this
enquiring was necessary at all — must the chief clerk come himself, and must the whole innocent family
be shown thereby that the investigation of this suspicious business could be entrusted only to the chief
clerk's understanding? And more as a result of the agitation into which these reflections put him than
of any proper decision, he swung himself with all his might out of the bed. There was a loud thump, but
not an actual crash. The carpet softened the fall a little, and his back was more elastic than Gregor
had thought, which accounted for the not very noticeable dull sound. Only he had not held his head
carefully enough and had knocked it; he turned it and rubbed it on the carpet in vexation and pain.

"Something fell in there," said the chief clerk in the room on the left. Gregor tried to imagine whether
something similar to what had happened to him today might not one day happen to the chief clerk; one
really had to admit the possibility. But as if in brutal answer to the question, the chief clerk now
took a few decided steps in the next room and made his patent-leather boots creak. From the room on the
right his sister whispered, to let Gregor know: "Gregor, the chief clerk is here." "I know," Gregor said
to himself; but he did not dare raise his voice loud enough for his sister to hear.

"Gregor," his father now said from the room on the left, "the chief clerk has come and wishes to know
why you did not leave by the early train. We do not know what to tell him. Besides, he wants to speak to
you personally. So please open the door. He will be good enough to excuse the untidiness of the room."
"Good morning, Herr Samsa," the chief clerk called in amiably. "He is not well," said his mother to the
chief clerk while his father was still talking at the door, "he is not well, believe me, Herr Prokurist.
How else would Gregor miss a train! The boy has nothing in his head but the business. It almost annoys
me that he never goes out in the evening; he has been in town a whole week now, and he was at home every
evening. He sits there with us at the table reading the paper quietly or studying timetables. It is
quite a diversion for him to do a little fretwork. He carved a small frame, for instance, in the course
of two or three evenings; you will be amazed how pretty it is; it hangs inside the room; you will see it
at once when Gregor opens up. I am glad, by the way, that you are here, Herr Prokurist; on our own we
should never have got Gregor to open the door; he is so stubborn; and he is certainly not well, though
he denied it this morning." "I am coming at once," said Gregor slowly and deliberately, and did not
stir, so as not to miss a word of the conversation. "I cannot explain it to myself any other way,
madam," said the chief clerk; "I hope it is nothing serious. Though on the other hand I must say that we
businessmen — luckily or unluckily, as you please — very often simply have to overcome a slight
indisposition out of regard for business." "So can the chief clerk come in to you now?" asked his
impatient father, knocking at the door again. "No," said Gregor. In the room on the left an awkward
silence fell; in the room on the right his sister began to sob.

Why did his sister not go to the others? She had probably only just got out of bed and had not yet begun
to dress. And why was she crying? Because he was not getting up and not letting the chief clerk in,
because he was in danger of losing his post, and because the chief would then pursue his parents again
with the old claims? Those were surely, for the time being, needless worries. Gregor was still here and
had not the slightest thought of abandoning his family. At the moment he was lying there on the carpet,
to be sure, and no one who knew his condition would have seriously demanded that he let the chief clerk
in. But for this small discourtesy, for which a suitable excuse could easily be found later, Gregor
could hardly be dismissed on the spot. And it seemed to Gregor that it would be far more sensible to
leave him in peace now than to disturb him with weeping and entreaty. But it was just the uncertainty
that pressed upon the others and excused their behaviour.
